# **Cell-Context–Dependent Transcriptomic Connectivity of High-Concentration Montelukast in Atopic Dermatitis: Comparison With CysLT1R Perturbation and Implications for Topical Repurposing**

## **Abstract**

Atopic dermatitis (AD) can remain symptomatic despite targeted treatment, and evidence does not support routine use of oral montelukast at 10 mg daily for AD. This exploratory study asked whether a high-concentration montelukast signature differed from signatures of cysteinyl leukotriene type 1 receptor (CysLT1R) perturbation and whether cell-line selection affected its apparent alignment with AD skin. A 23,468-gene consensus from four human skin cohorts was compared with LINCS L1000 profiles using weighted connectivity scores (WTCS), 2,000 gene-set permutations, and Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment. Drug signatures were also compared with skin-biopsy responses to crisaborole and dupilumab.

All 26 high-concentration montelukast profiles were generated at 10 µM; 19 came from the carcinoma comparison subset. The pooled signature was concordant with global AD expression (WTCS \= \+0.3169), whereas the seven-profile subset spanning A375, A549, and HA1E showed directional reversal (WTCS \= −0.2499; false discovery rate \[FDR\] \= 0.3826). The restricted subset also showed negative connectivity on the combined cAMP and arachidonic acid panel (WTCS \= −0.2955; nominal *p* \= 0.0285; FDR \= 0.1209). Pooled montelukast scores on that panel differed from CysLT1R comparators by −0.2523 to −0.5434. On the same panel, montelukast ranked second by cosine similarity to the day-15 crisaborole-versus-vehicle response (+0.2031), behind roflumilast (+0.2145). Across all shared genes, montelukast showed small positive rank correlations with day-15 crisaborole and week-16 dupilumab responses.

The results distinguish two patterns of transcriptomic connectivity, not two proven mechanisms in skin. They support testing whether a localized formulation can achieve measurable exposure and pharmacologic activity in viable AD skin without substantial systemic absorption.

## **1\. Introduction**

### **1.1 Present Clinical Challenges in Atopic Dermatitis**

Dupilumab and topical phosphodiesterase 4 (PDE4) inhibitors offer different ways to treat AD without relying solely on topical corticosteroids. Dupilumab binds the interleukin-4 receptor alpha subunit (IL-4Rα), which is shared by receptor complexes involved in IL-4 and IL-13 signaling. In a randomized biopsy study, treatment progressively shifted lesional skin expression toward a nonlesional pattern. Topical crisaborole likewise shifted inflammatory and barrier biomarkers in patient skin, while the INTEGUMENT-1 and INTEGUMENT-2 trials demonstrated clinical improvement with roflumilast cream compared with vehicle. These advances do not eliminate the cost or practical burden of injectable therapy, and incomplete control leaves room for accessible nonsteroidal options, particularly for patients with persistent itchy plaques (Bissonnette et al., 2019; Guttman-Yassky et al., 2019; Sanofi-Aventis U.S. LLC, 2026, § 12.1; Simpson et al., 2024; Zimmermann et al., 2018). ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31419544/?utm_source=openai))

Chronic AD is not simply an acute rash that lasts longer. Biopsy studies show increasing Th2 and Th22 activity accompanied by epidermal hyperplasia and changes in differentiation. Abnormal epidermal lipids and defects in tight-junction proteins such as claudin-1 compromise the barrier. Periostin is another candidate marker: experimental studies implicate it in signaling between fibroblasts and keratinocytes and in the activation of itch-sensing neurons. These processes provide a rationale for examining inflammatory, remodeling, and barrier markers separately rather than treating a single expression score as a complete measure of disease activity (Berdyshev et al., 2018; De Benedetto et al., 2011; Gittler et al., 2012; Masuoka et al., 2012; Mishra et al., 2020). ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29467325/?utm_source=openai))

### **1.2 The Clinical-Pharmacological Paradox of Oral Montelukast**

The clinical evidence for oral montelukast in AD is mixed. Small studies reported improvement, but two double-blind adult trials found no significant benefit over placebo with 10 mg daily, administered for four or eight weeks. A Cochrane review found no convincing evidence of improvement in eczema severity, itching, or topical corticosteroid use compared with placebo, while noting that the evidence was limited and of low quality. A broader 2025 meta-analysis likewise found no significant overall effect, despite favorable results in some subgroups and considerable heterogeneity. These findings do not support routine use for AD, and they do not show that higher doses would work (Ferguson et al., 2018; Friedmann et al., 2007; Mahmood et al., 2025; Nettis et al., 2002; Veien et al., 2005). ([cochrane.org](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD011224_are-non-steroidal-anti-inflammatory-medicines-known-leukotriene-receptor-antagonists-helpful-and))

Montelukast is a high-affinity antagonist of CysLT1R, and the prescribing information documents functional antagonism in the human airway. Oral pharmacokinetics, however, raise a separate question about additional targets. The October 2026 U.S. prescribing information reports a mean adult peak total plasma concentration of 353 ng/mL, approximately 0.60 µM, and more than 99% plasma protein binding. At that mean total peak, an unbound fraction below 1% implies an unbound plasma concentration below approximately 6 nM. This is a calculation from plasma data, not a measurement of free drug or receptor occupancy in lesional skin. It also cannot be equated directly with a nominal concentration added to culture medium (Lynch et al., 1999; National Center for Biotechnology Information, n.d.; Organon LLC, 2026, §§ 12.1–12.3). ([nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/21658?utm_source=openai))

Two lines of experimental evidence make an exposure-dependent hypothesis plausible. Anderson et al. (2009) observed increased intracellular cAMP in isolated human neutrophils at 1 µM and found that montelukast inhibited both cAMP and cGMP hydrolysis in neutrophil extracts, with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration of 3.4 µM for cAMP hydrolysis. The accompanying reductions in calcium influx and inflammatory readouts were consistent with a secondary action beyond conventional leukotriene receptor antagonism in that assay. Ramires et al. (2004) reported lower-micromolar effects on leukotriene production in cellular models and weaker direct inhibition of recombinant human 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX), with a reported half-maximal inhibitory concentration of approximately 30–50 µM. Neither study demonstrates selective PDE4 inhibition or engagement of either target in human AD skin. ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2697768/?utm_source=openai))

### **1.3 Study Rationale & Objectives**

Study 1 v2 asked whether a nominally high-concentration montelukast profile resembled the response to CysLT1R perturbation or showed a different pattern on genes associated with cAMP signaling and arachidonic acid metabolism. The analysis compared montelukast with genetic and chemical CysLT1R comparators, reference PDE4 inhibitors, and a 5-LOX inhibitor. Skin-biopsy responses to crisaborole and dupilumab provided a second point of comparison. The intent was to identify similarities worth testing in skin, not to infer equivalent therapeutic effects from similarity scores.

The second objective was to determine how pooling cell lines affected the result. LINCS L1000 profiles capture transcription after perturbation, but the response to the same compound can depend on the cell line in which it is measured (Subramanian et al., 2017). The analysis therefore scored the pooled profile and the two predefined cell-line subsets separately. It treated the cAMP and arachidonic acid panel as a transcriptional readout, not a direct assay of intracellular cAMP, PDE activity, or lipid metabolism. The master report dated November 26, 2026, is the source of the numerical findings reported below (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026). ([doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2017.10.049?utm_source=openai))

## **2\. Methods**

### **2.1 Standardized AD Pathway & Sub-Module Construction**

Four gene modules were defined in the analysis code before scoring. Human annotations were retrieved from the Enrichr 2025 Gene Ontology Biological Process library, QuickGO descendant annotations, KEGG, and Reactome. Using database-defined membership reduced reliance on handpicked marker lists, although the choice of pathways remained an analytical decision (Binns et al., 2009; Kanehisa & Goto, 2000; Kuleshov et al., 2016; Milacic et al., 2024). ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19744993/?utm_source=openai))

The type 2 inflammation module combined GO:0042092 from QuickGO descendants; GO:0002828 from Enrichr; KEGG hsa04658 and hsa04664, covering Th1/Th2 differentiation and FcεRI signaling; and Reactome R-HSA-6785807, covering IL-4 and IL-13 signaling. The epidermal barrier module comprised GO:0061436, GO:0031424 from QuickGO descendants, GO:0030216, GO:0008544, KEGG hsa04530, hsa00062, and hsa00600, and Reactome R-HSA-6805567 for keratinization. The antimicrobial peptide and innate immunity module combined GO:0019730, GO:0061844, GO:0019731, and the KEGG IL-17 signaling pathway, hsa04657. The module labeled `cAMP_PDE4_5LOX_Panel` was the union of two broad KEGG maps: cAMP signaling (hsa04024) and arachidonic acid metabolism (hsa00590). The label describes the question the panel was designed to address; its membership is not specific to PDE4 or 5-LOX activity (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, § 1). ([kegg.jp](https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html?utm_source=openai))

### **2.2 Multi-Cohort Human AD Consensus Signature & Clinical Benchmarks**

Four Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) series supplied lesional and nonlesional skin profiles. GSE130588 was a GPL570 microarray study of dupilumab with baseline, week-4, and week-16 biopsies and SCORing Atopic Dermatitis (SCORAD) data. GSE133477 used the same platform in an intrapatient trial that randomized lesions to crisaborole or vehicle, with baseline, optional day-8, and day-15 biopsies. GSE121212 supplied bulk RNA sequencing from a study of AD, psoriasis, and healthy skin; only AD skin samples were used here. GSE157194 supplied bulk RNA sequencing from the independent TREATgermany registry cohort, which included three-month follow-up after dupilumab or cyclosporine (Bissonnette et al., 2019; Guttman-Yassky et al., 2019; Möbus et al., 2021; Tsoi et al., 2019). ([ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE130588&utm_source=openai))

The pipeline parsed structured sample characteristics, using titles and labeled characteristics for fallback group assignment rather than free-text descriptions. For microarrays, it mapped probes to gene symbols, retained the highest-variance probe for each symbol, and applied a log₂ transformation only when the expression scale met the code’s threshold for doing so. RNA-sequencing counts were converted to log₂ counts per million plus one. Contrasts used differences in group means, unpaired Welch tests, and gene-level Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment. The disease consensus averaged cohort-level log₂ fold changes and *t* statistics for genes represented in at least two cohorts. This inclusion criterion did not require significant differential expression or agreement in direction. Of 23,468 consensus genes, 11,955 were represented in the L1000 vectors, including inferred transcripts. The global query used the 150 highest-ranked AD-up and 150 highest-ranked AD-down genes by mean *t* statistic within each direction (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, §§ 3–4). ([onlinelibrary.wiley.com](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1995.tb02031.x?utm_source=openai))

Clinical contrasts were computed separately: crisaborole at days 8 and 15 versus day 1, crisaborole versus vehicle at day 15, and dupilumab at weeks 4 and 16 versus baseline. In TREATgermany, the code compared 21 month-3 dupilumab lesional samples with the full 57-sample lesional baseline group, not just the corresponding pretreatment biopsies. For the SCORAD check, the pipeline correlated mean module expression with recorded severity across 188 biopsy records from GSE130588. That count represents samples, not independent participants (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, §§ 3, 9). ([ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSM3744205&utm_source=openai))

### **2.3 LINCS L1000 Retrieval, Comparator Proxies & Tissue Stratification**

The pipeline queried SigCom LINCS by compound name, configured Broad perturbagen identifiers, and, where available, canonical SMILES. It took the union of the results and removed duplicate signature IDs. The public Broad L1000 Phase I and II releases are catalogued as GSE92742 and GSE70138, respectively. The analysis retrieved records through SigCom LINCS rather than directly from those releases, so the GEO series identify the underlying platform resources, not the provenance of every profile used. Up to 100 signatures per group were selected in deterministic ID order. For each gene, the pipeline took the median across successfully retrieved profiles and standardized the resulting consensus vector across genes (Evangelista et al., 2022; Subramanian et al., 2017; *Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, § 2). ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35524556/?utm_source=openai))

The low bin was defined as 1 µM or less and the high bin as 10 µM or more. No low-concentration montelukast profiles were retrieved. There was one profile at 6.66 µM in HUH7, one record with an unknown concentration, and 26 profiles at exactly 10 µM. The predefined fallback therefore used `CYSLTR1_KD`, `Pranlukast_Low`, `Pranlukast_All`, `Zafirlukast_Low`, and `Zafirlukast_All`. Genetic perturbation is not equivalent to a specific concentration of receptor antagonist. Nor are the chemical comparators target-exclusive: some CysLT1R antagonists can also interfere with nucleotide signaling through P2Y receptors. Reference drugs included zileuton for 5-LOX and roflumilast, crisaborole, rolipram, and apremilast for PDE4 (Mamedova et al., 2005; *Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, § 2). ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4967539/?utm_source=openai))

The predefined `RelevantTissue` panel included epithelial, cutaneous, and immune-lineage models, but only three lines contributed montelukast profiles: A375 melanoma, A549 lung carcinoma, and HA1E immortalized kidney epithelial cells. These contributed seven profiles. The remaining 19 comprised HCC515, HT29, MCF7, PC3, and VCAP and were labeled `HormoneTumor` in the pipeline. That label is shorthand for a selected carcinoma group; it does not imply that all five lines are hormone-driven. The corresponding comparator subsets were not necessarily composed of the same lines or exposure conditions (Broad Institute Connectivity Map, n.d.; *Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, § 2). ([assets.clue.io](https://assets.clue.io/resources/sop-cell.pdf?utm_source=openai))

### **2.4 Connectivity Scoring, Clinical Alignment & Statistical Rigor**

The pipeline calculated separate weighted enrichment scores for AD-up and AD-down genes in each ranked perturbation profile. The raw connectivity score was half their difference. Following the Connectivity Map convention, the two-sided WTCS was set to zero when both enrichment scores had the same sign. Negative WTCS indicated that the perturbation opposed the disease query; a zero WTCS did not necessarily mean that neither set was enriched. The empirical *p* value was calculated against the raw statistic using 2,000 size-matched random gene-set draws. Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment was applied across perturbagen-by-module tests and separately across individual pathway tests. These tests evaluate enrichment against random gene sets; they do not estimate uncertainty across cell lines or directly test differences between strata (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995; Subramanian et al., 2017). ([onlinelibrary.wiley.com](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1995.tb02031.x?utm_source=openai))

For clinical alignment, the pipeline reported cosine similarity and Spearman rank correlation across all shared genes (`Full_Overlap`), the top 500 AD consensus genes, the union of the three core AD modules, and each individual module. Clinical FDR values apply to the Spearman tests, not to cosine rankings. Individual transcript signals were ranked by the product of the AD consensus log₂ fold change and the montelukast L1000 *Z* score. Opposite signs yielded a negative product. This ranking was descriptive: an L1000 *Z* score is not a log₂ fold change or a gene-level significance test. The analysis did not separate directly measured L1000 landmark genes from inferred transcripts (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, §§ 5, 9, 11–12; Subramanian et al., 2017). ([doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2017.10.049?utm_source=openai))

## **3\. Results**

### **3.1 Validation of Standardized AD Consensus Signature & Clinical Modules**

All 19 pathway terms returned gene sets. The type 2, epidermal barrier, innate immunity, and cAMP/arachidonic acid modules contained 264, 562, 229, and 289 genes, respectively, before restriction to the signed disease query and L1000 overlap. In GSE130588, the innate immunity and type 2 module scores correlated positively with SCORAD across 188 records (Spearman ρ \= 0.5095 and 0.4409, respectively; both nominal *p* \< 0.00001 as reported at the available precision). The epidermal barrier and cAMP/arachidonic acid modules did not show comparable associations (ρ \= 0.0097 and −0.0860, respectively). The first two results provide an internal check on biological relevance, not independent validation: the cohort contributed to the disease signature, and patients could contribute multiple biopsies (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, §§ 1, 3–4).

### **3.2 Cell Context and the Pan-Cell Sign Inversion**

The pooled 10 µM montelukast profile was concordant with the global AD signature (WTCS \= \+0.3169, FDR \= 0.0227) and with the innate immunity module (+0.3491, FDR \= 0.0773). The carcinoma subset, which contributed 73% of the pooled profiles, had a similar global score (+0.3181, FDR \= 0.0227). In the seven-profile subset, the global score changed sign (−0.2499, FDR \= 0.3826), and the raw innate immunity score fell from \+0.3289 in the carcinoma subset to \+0.0231, corresponding to a WTCS of zero. The combined cAMP/arachidonic acid panel was more negative in the restricted subset (WTCS \= −0.2955; nominal *p* \= 0.0285; FDR \= 0.1209) than in the pooled analysis (−0.2523; FDR \= 0.3961). Neither negative montelukast result met a 5% FDR threshold (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, §§ 2, 5, 8).

Roflumilast showed a similar change on the global query, from \+0.2700 in the carcinoma subset to −0.3330 in its restricted subset (FDR \= 0.0227). In that restricted subset, it also had negative connectivity on the cAMP/arachidonic acid panel (−0.3199, FDR \= 0.0493). Rolipram behaved differently. Its pooled global score was \+0.3431, and its restricted global score remained positive (+0.2821). Its innate immunity raw score, however, changed from \+0.0573 in the carcinoma subset to −0.3145 in the restricted subset (nominal *p* \= 0.0425; FDR \= 0.1609). Thus, the effect of stratification depended on both the drug and the gene set. The analysis showed a reversal in the direction of some scores, not a formal drug-by-cell-context interaction (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, §§ 5, 8).

### **3.3 Mechanistic Separation: CysLT1R Comparators and the cAMP/Arachidonic Acid Signature**

The restricted `CYSLTR1_KD` profile had the most negative global AD score among the screened signatures (−0.3465, FDR \= 0.0208). The epidermal barrier score was also negative (−0.3091, FDR \= 0.0337), while the type 2 score was directionally negative but did not meet a 5% FDR threshold (−0.3164, FDR \= 0.0842). `Pranlukast_All` showed negative global and barrier connectivity (−0.3147, FDR \= 0.0227; and −0.3334, FDR \= 0.0156, respectively). In contrast, the restricted montelukast profile had a WTCS of zero on both the type 2 and epidermal barrier modules. The comparator findings therefore identify an AD-related pattern associated with CysLT1R perturbation without showing that 10 µM montelukast reproduces it (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, §§ 5, 7–8).

On the cAMP/arachidonic acid panel, pooled `CYSLTR1_KD`, `Pranlukast_Low`, and `Zafirlukast_All` scored \+0.2911, \+0.2704, and \+0.2688, while `Pranlukast_All` and `Zafirlukast_Low` both had a WTCS of zero. Pooled montelukast scored −0.2523, a difference of −0.2523 to −0.5434 relative to the comparators. These were descriptive differences, not formal tests of target independence. The constituent pathways also differed by subset. The cAMP signaling raw score was −0.3291 in the restricted subset, but the arachidonic acid metabolism score of −0.3314 belonged to the pooled profile; its restricted score was \+0.0111. Other restricted-subset raw scores were −0.4274 for fatty acid elongation, −0.2887 for Th1/Th2 differentiation, and −0.2797 for FcεRI signaling. Fatty acid elongation and FcεRI signaling had been positive in the pooled profile (+0.3688 and \+0.2585). These values describe the direction of expression connectivity, not inhibition of the pathways themselves (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, §§ 5–7). ([kegg.jp](https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html?utm_source=openai))

### **3.4 Alignment With Clinical Crisaborole and Dupilumab Skin Biopsies**

Across 9,630–11,870 shared genes, the restricted montelukast profile showed weak positive correlations with the clinical signatures. For crisaborole at day 15 versus baseline, cosine similarity was \+0.0410 and Spearman ρ was \+0.0444 (FDR \= 0.00017). For crisaborole versus vehicle at day 15, both values were \+0.0432 (Spearman FDR \= 0.00031). For dupilumab, cosine similarity and ρ were \+0.0217 and \+0.0261 at week 16 (FDR \= 0.0383), and \+0.0222 and \+0.0253 at week 4 (FDR \= 0.0473). Day-8 crisaborole also showed positive rank alignment (ρ \= \+0.0361, FDR \= 0.00431), whereas month-3 dupilumab did not meet the 5% FDR threshold (ρ \= \+0.0230, FDR \= 0.0702). The carcinoma montelukast profile had negative cosine similarity across all six comparisons, but its negative rank correlation with week-16 dupilumab was not significant. The small correlation coefficients are more informative about the magnitude of agreement than the adjusted *p* values alone (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, § 9).

On the 164 shared genes in the cAMP/arachidonic acid panel, restricted montelukast ranked second by cosine similarity to the day-15 crisaborole-versus-vehicle response (+0.2031), behind restricted roflumilast (+0.2145) and ahead of zileuton (+0.1986) and L1000 crisaborole (+0.0583). Montelukast’s accompanying Spearman FDR was 0.4014, compared with 0.0336 for roflumilast. Direct full-profile comparison between the restricted montelukast and roflumilast L1000 signatures yielded a cosine of only \+0.0544. The resemblance was therefore specific to a selected panel and benchmark, not a close match across the full profiles (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, §§ 9–10).

The restricted `CYSLTR1_KD` profile ranked first by cosine similarity on the top-500 AD gene set for all six benchmarks. Its cosines were \+0.2200 for crisaborole day 8, \+0.2166 for day 15, and \+0.2299 for day 15 versus vehicle. For dupilumab, they were \+0.1843 at week 4, \+0.2069 at week 16, and \+0.2234 at month 3\. The corresponding Spearman FDR values were 0.00156, 0.00380, 0.00222, 0.01517, 0.00591, and 0.00591. This first-place ranking did not extend to every other gene universe (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, § 9).

### **3.5 Gene-Level Transcriptional Drivers**

The gene-level results came from the pooled 10 µM profile, not the seven-profile subset. Several signals opposed the AD consensus. Montelukast *Z* scores and corresponding AD log₂ fold changes were −1.869 versus \+2.676 for *MMP12*, −2.782 versus \+2.147 for *KRT6A*, −1.581 versus \+3.025 for *KRT16*, −1.335 versus \+1.595 for *KRT6B*, −1.060 versus \+1.559 for *KRT17*, and −4.391 versus \+0.314 for *KRT14*. The same pattern was seen for *SPRR1A* (−2.515 versus \+1.616) and *SPRR1B* (−1.246 versus \+1.404). Other opposing signals included *SCEL* (+1.607 versus −0.977), *ELOVL6* (+1.329 versus −0.512), and *CLDN1* (+0.549 versus −0.580). *POSTN* had a strongly negative montelukast score (−3.045), but its AD consensus value was near zero and slightly negative (−0.024); it is a candidate marker, not an example of disease reversal in this analysis. *FLG* and *LOR* were negative in both profiles, providing counterexamples to a generalized barrier-restoration claim (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, §§ 11–12).

The pooled profile also showed lower signals for *IL4R* (−1.564 versus AD \+1.159), *IL13RA2* (−1.768 versus \+1.310), *IL13* (−0.345 versus \+0.434), *HLA-DQA1* (−2.525 versus \+0.666), *HLA-DRA* (−1.886 versus \+0.527), and *PLA2G2A* (−1.684 versus \+1.223). Conversely, *S100A8*, *S100A9*, and *S100A12* had positive scores of \+2.388, \+2.711, and \+3.468, respectively; *CXCL8* and *WNT5A* were also positive (+2.178 and \+3.907). The attenuation of the innate immunity score in the restricted subset does not establish attenuation of each individual gene. Likewise, positive *NR4A1* and *DUSP1* scores (+2.655 and \+1.534) do not demonstrate cAMP-dependent stress signaling. *ALOX5* and *ALOX15* had positive scores of \+2.535 and \+1.138, while *ALOX5* was negative for zileuton and roflumilast (−0.768 and −2.784). These measurements do not establish the direction of enzyme activity or lipid production (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, §§ 8, 11–12).

## **4\. Discussion**

### **4.1 Most Promising Translation: Localized/Topical High-Concentration Montelukast**

The most direct translational question is whether montelukast can reach viable skin at a concentration that changes the proposed pharmacologic readouts. More than 99% plasma protein binding and the submicromolar mean total plasma peak after a standard oral dose make exposure a plausible constraint. But the oral AD trials did not measure lesional montelukast concentrations, PDE inhibition, or 5-LOX activity. The present data cannot establish why those trials produced mixed results. Nor was 10 µM an activation threshold: it was the only concentration represented in the high bin, and prior neutrophil experiments detected some effects at lower nominal concentrations (Anderson et al., 2009; Friedmann et al., 2007; Organon LLC, 2026, § 12.3; Ramires et al., 2004; Veien et al., 2005). ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2697768/?utm_source=openai))

The boxed warning is another reason not to extrapolate these findings into higher oral dosing. The FDA and the October 2026 prescribing information advise immediate discontinuation and contact with a healthcare provider if new neuropsychiatric symptoms, behavioral changes, or suicidal thoughts or behavior occur. The label also notes that the underlying mechanism is not well understood and that the risk is difficult to quantify. It does not establish a dose–risk relationship for the exposures proposed here (Organon LLC, 2026, § 5.1; U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2020). ([organon.com](https://www.organon.com/product/usa/pi_circulars/s/singulair/singulair_pi.pdf))

A skin-directed formulation could deliver drug without first passing through the oral absorption and hepatic first-pass route. It would not eliminate binding within skin or plasma, and systemic absorption would remain possible. Crisaborole and roflumilast provide precedents for local delivery in AD, not proof that montelukast will penetrate or be safe when delivered in the same way. Topical montelukast formulations have already been described in a U.S. patent, including diffusion-cell and mouse experiments. The unresolved question is whether a formulation can deliver sufficient **unbound drug to viable human AD skin**, with measurable pharmacologic effects, acceptable local tolerability, and limited systemic exposure. Franz diffusion-cell studies and viable human skin experiments can address the first parts of that question; systemic pharmacokinetic and safety studies would still be needed (Avramoff et al., 2020; Bissonnette et al., 2019; Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2004; Simpson et al., 2024). ([patents.google.com](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10548837B1/en?utm_source=openai))

### **4.2 Mechanistic Insights & Candidate Clinical Phenotypes for Trial Design**

The results support a two-axis hypothesis. CysLT1R knockdown and pranlukast showed the clearest negative connectivity on the global and barrier queries. Montelukast showed a different pattern on the combined cAMP and arachidonic acid panel, with the most negative cAMP signaling score in the restricted subset but the negative arachidonic acid metabolism score in the pooled profile. Lower pooled *PLA2G2A* signal adds a candidate readout because the gene encodes a secreted phospholipase that can contribute to phospholipid hydrolysis and lipid-mediator production. It does not establish direct enzyme inhibition or reduced arachidonic acid release. A mechanistic test would ask whether the montelukast response persists after CysLT1R loss in the same cell background and whether it is accompanied by changes in cyclic nucleotides and lipid mediators (Anderson et al., 2009; Murakami et al., 2015; Ramires et al., 2004). ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2697768/?utm_source=openai))

Chronic lichenified plaques are a reasonable setting in which to test the proposed markers, not an identified responder phenotype. *MMP12*, *KRT6A*, *KRT16*, and *SPRR1A* can be examined alongside histologic epidermal thickness, while periostin could be assessed as an exploratory itch and remodeling marker. The limits of cross-model comparisons are particularly clear for *MMP12*: the L1000 scores were \+1.625 for crisaborole and \+1.514 for zileuton, compared with −1.869 for montelukast. Yet crisaborole reduced *MMP12* in patient skin at both days 8 and 15\. The L1000 ranking therefore does not establish clinical superiority. Likewise, *CLDN1* and fatty acid elongation genes suggest testing tight-junction function and epidermal lipid composition rather than assuming that higher transcript signals restore the barrier (Berdyshev et al., 2018; Bissonnette et al., 2019; De Benedetto et al., 2011; Gittler et al., 2012; Masuoka et al., 2012; Mishra et al., 2020). ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29467325/?utm_source=openai))

The type 2 findings also warrant restraint. IL-13Rα1, not IL-13Rα2, pairs with IL-4Rα in the type II signaling receptor. IL-13Rα2 can attenuate IL-13 responses, so a lower *IL13RA2* transcript signal cannot be read as weaker IL-13 signaling. Lower *HLA-DQA1* and *HLA-DRA* signals do not establish reduced antigen presentation. Moreover, *CCL17* and *CCL18* were positive in the pooled montelukast profile (+0.749 and \+0.579), whereas they were negative after dupilumab in the clinical benchmark. The data do not establish a role for montelukast as an adjunct, bridge, or steroid-sparing maintenance therapy, nor do they identify people with allergic comorbidities as preferential responders. The *ALOX5* and *ALOX15* findings justify measuring leukotrienes and other eicosanoids, not predicting a withdrawal flare or recommending a taper. The Singulair label reports no rebound worsening in the adult asthma withdrawal studies it describes; those studies do not answer the question in AD (Chandriani et al., 2014; Guttman-Yassky et al., 2019; Organon LLC, 2026, § 14.1). ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24879793/?utm_source=openai))

### **4.3 Methodological Contribution: Tissue-Context Stratification in L1000 Repurposing**

The clearest methodological finding is that changing the cell-line composition changed the interpretation of the same 10 µM exposure. Pooling produced a positive global montelukast score; separating the profiles uncovered a negative estimate in a smaller subset. The pooled result should not be dismissed as erroneous: it describes the retrieved mixture of profiles, and the restricted result describes a different mixture. Roflumilast showed a similar global sign change, but rolipram did not. The evidence supports checking the sensitivity of a repurposing result to cell selection, not a general rule that carcinoma pooling systematically inverts PDE4-active drugs (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, §§ 5, 8; Subramanian et al., 2017). ([doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2017.10.049?utm_source=openai))

Nor does the restricted subset reproduce an AD lesion. One line is melanoma, one is lung carcinoma, and one is immortalized kidney epithelium. The cultures do not capture the architecture of an epidermal barrier or the interactions among lesional keratinocytes, fibroblasts, T cells, mast cells, and dendritic cells. The median across available profiles can also give more influence to lines with more profiles. A stronger sensitivity analysis would balance contributions by line, match compounds on dose and exposure time where possible, and test whether the result survives the removal of any one line (Broad Institute Connectivity Map, n.d.; Subramanian et al., 2017; Tsoi et al., 2019). ([assets.clue.io](https://assets.clue.io/resources/sop-cell.pdf?utm_source=openai))

### **4.4 Limitations & Next Steps**

The absence of montelukast signatures at 1 µM or below precluded a within-compound dose-response analysis. The single 6.66 µM profile came from a different cell background and did not repair that gap. The restricted cAMP/arachidonic acid result was nominally significant but not FDR significant, and the restricted global result had an FDR of 0.3826. A formal between-stratum comparison should use individual profiles, account for the repeated contribution of cell lines, and report uncertainty for the difference itself. The clinical benchmarks also need a patient-aware sensitivity analysis: the reanalysis used unpaired contrasts even when the source design involved repeated biopsies or intrapatient treatment assignment. The month-3 registry comparison used a broader baseline group, and some cohorts contributed to both disease-signature construction and clinical benchmarking. Patient-level paired contrasts and leave-one-cohort-out disease signatures would provide a stronger test of generalizability (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, §§ 2–5, 9). ([doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1016%2Fj.jaci.2019.06.047?utm_source=openai))

The next study could use single-cell and spatial transcriptomics to map *CYSLTR1*, *PDE4B*, *PDE4D*, *ALOX5*, *IL4R*, and *POSTN* across keratinocyte, stromal, and immune compartments in lesional skin. An ex vivo follow-up could then test montelukast across a nominal range such as 0.1–30 µM in viable human skin models, measuring tissue exposure, intracellular cAMP, 5-LOX products and other lipid mediators, and barrier proteins and function. That range is a proposed laboratory exposure series, not a topical formulation strength or a clinical dose. Matched vehicle and reference-drug controls, together with viability measurements, would help distinguish the proposed pharmacology from nonspecific cell stress. The exact SigCom signature IDs, source collections, cell lines, durations, retained and excluded records, and landmark-versus-inferred annotations should accompany the code and output files. The existing master report and script document the analysis, but the report does not itself supply a complete per-signature manifest (*Study 1 v2 Master Report*, 2026, §§ 2, 13–14). ([doi.org](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2017.10.049?utm_source=openai))

## **5\. Conclusion**

At a nominal concentration of 10 µM, montelukast produced different AD connectivity scores depending on the L1000 cell lines included. The pooled profile was concordant with global AD expression, whereas a smaller, predefined subset yielded a negative global estimate and a stronger directional signal on the cAMP/arachidonic acid panel. The latter differed from the CysLT1R comparators and showed limited similarity to skin-biopsy responses to crisaborole and dupilumab. The principal negative montelukast connectivity scores did not survive FDR correction, and the individual cAMP and arachidonic acid pathways did not behave alike across subsets.

The findings justify testing whether the candidate signals can be reproduced in human AD skin at measured local exposures. They do not establish selective PDE4 inhibition, CysLT1R independence, a therapeutic concentration threshold, or efficacy at higher oral doses. The case for a localized formulation rests on a question that can be answered experimentally: whether viable skin can receive enough unbound drug to produce a reproducible pharmacologic effect while keeping systemic exposure low. Until that is shown, topical montelukast remains a repurposing hypothesis rather than a steroid-sparing treatment.
